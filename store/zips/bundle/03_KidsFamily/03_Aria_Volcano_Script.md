### WHAT IS A VOLCANO? (0:45–3:30)

[VISUAL CUE: Aria sits cross-legged on the floor, animated diagrams appear behind her on a whiteboard or green screen]

Okay so — raise your hand if you've ever wondered what the inside of the Earth looks like.

*(Aria raises her own hand enthusiastically)*
ME. I have. Every time I drop something and it goes under my bed and I have to crawl to get it, I'm like, what is DOWN here? What's at the bottom?

[B-ROLL: Animated cross-section of Earth — outer layers peeling back to reveal the core]

So here's the thing. The Earth has layers. Like a really, really big jawbreaker. On the outside, you've got the **crust** — that's the part we live on. Solid. Hard. Like the ground you're standing on right now.

But go deeper, and it gets... wild.

Under the crust, there's the **mantle**. And the mantle is SO hot — we're talking thousands of degrees — that rock actually melts. Melted rock. Like if you took your crayons and left them on the sidewalk on a really hot summer day and they turned all gloopy?

[VISUAL CUE: Fun animated clip of crayons melting — Aria makes a "melting" face with sound effect]

That gloopy melted rock is called **MAGMA** when it's still underground. But — and this is SO important — when it comes OUT of a volcano? It has a totally different name. It's called **LAVA**.

[TEXT OVERLAY on screen: "MAGMA = underground 🔴 LAVA = above ground 🌋"]

*(Aria wags her finger)*
Magma. Lava. Two names for the same stuff, depending on where it is. Don't mix them up or your science teacher will give you THAT look. You know the look.

[VISUAL CUE: Aria does a funny impression of a disapproving teacher face — kids will love this]

Okay, so — why does this magma come out in the first place? Why are there volcanoes?

So imagine the Earth's crust is like a giant puzzle. But the pieces don't stay still — they're always moving, like, super super slowly. We call these pieces **tectonic plates**.

[VISUAL CUE: Animated puzzle pieces — floating on what looks like bubbling magma below]

Sometimes the puzzle pieces push INTO each other. And when that happens, one piece gets pushed down... down... into that hot melted rock zone. And the heat and the pressure build up, and the magma has to go SOMEWHERE. So it finds a weak spot in the crust, and it pushes up, and UP, and... BOOM. Volcano.

Pretty wild, right?

Now there are actually different *kinds* of volcanoes. Two big ones you should know:

**Shield volcanoes** — [VISUAL CUE: Wide, flat volcano animation] — these are wide and flat, like an upside-down warrior's shield. They happen when runny lava flows out slowly over a long time. Hawaii has shield volcanoes — that's how the Hawaiian islands were actually made! Lava piled up over millions of years until it broke the surface of the ocean.

**Stratovolcanoes** — [VISUAL CUE: Tall, pointy, classic-looking volcano] — these are the tall, pointy, classic-looking volcanoes. They're made of layers of ash and lava that built up over thousands of eruptions. These are the dramatic ones. Mount St. Helens in Washington? Stratovolcano. Mount Fuji in Japan? Stratovolcano. These are the ones you don't want to live too close to.

[VISUAL CUE: Aria comparing heights with her hands — one low and spread wide (shield), one shooting straight up (stratovolcano)]

**FACT NUMBER TWO.**

[TEXT OVERLAY: "🔥 FACT #2: Lava Temperature"]

*(Aria's eyes go huge)*

**FACT NUMBER THREE.**

[TEXT OVERLAY: "🌊 FACT #3: Underwater Volcanoes!"]

There are volcanoes **under the ocean**. They're called **seamounts** and there are actually more underwater volcanoes than there are on land. The mid-ocean ridges — which are like giant underwater mountain chains — are basically one long continuous volcanic zone. Most of the ocean floor was made by underwater volcanic activity. The ocean floor was literally *built* by volcanoes. I didn't know this until like two months ago and I'm still not over it.

**FACT NUMBER FOUR.**

[TEXT OVERLAY: "🌍 FACT #4: The Ring of Fire"]

There's a zone around the Pacific Ocean called the **Ring of Fire**, and it contains **75% of all the volcanoes on Earth**. Seventy-five percent. It's this huge horseshoe shape that goes from New Zealand up through Asia, across to Alaska, and down the west coast of the Americas. It's called the Ring of Fire because it follows where a bunch of tectonic plates meet — and all that plate activity means a LOT of volcanic and earthquake activity.

### SCIENCE EXPLANATION (9:30–10:30)

[VISUAL CUE: Aria sits in front of a simple whiteboard or animated graphic. She's wiping her hands on a towel, still a little keyed up from the experiment.]

*(Aria)*
Okay. Science time. And I PROMISE this is just as cool as the explosion part.

So remember how I said baking soda is a **base** and vinegar is an **acid**? When a base and an acid mix together, they have what scientists call a *chemical reaction*. They don't just sit there together — they change each other. They produce something NEW.

[VISUAL CUE: Simple diagram — baking soda icon + vinegar icon = CO2 bubbles icon]

When baking soda and vinegar mix, they produce a gas called **carbon dioxide** — or CO2. And gases take up a LOT of space.

*(Aria holds her arms wide to show "big space")*

So all that CO2 gas is suddenly being created really fast inside our volcano — and it NEEDS somewhere to go. So it pushes the liquid UP and OUT, taking the soap bubbles and food coloring with it.

[VISUAL CUE: Simple animation of gas bubbles pushing liquid upward through a tube]

*(Aria)*
That's it! That's the whole thing! Gas pushed the liquid out! 

It's basically what happens in a real volcano too — except instead of CO2, real volcanoes have other gases, and instead of vinegar and baking soda, real volcanoes have impossibly hot magma under incredible pressure. But the BIG IDEA is the same: pressure builds up, and stuff gets pushed out.

*(Aria points finger guns at the camera)*
You just did real chemistry. You understood real geology. You are officially a junior volcanologist and that is a real word.
